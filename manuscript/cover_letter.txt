Dear Editor,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, "Delay Sensitivity in Stated Vaccine Choice: Evidence from a Discrete Choice Experiment in Wuhan, China," for consideration in the Journal of Behavioral Medicine.

**Summary:** This study examines how waiting time before vaccination influences vaccine preferences using a discrete choice experiment (DCE) administered to 1,027 adults in Wuhan, China. Waiting time was embedded directly as a vaccine attribute, allowing us to estimate how respondents trade off waiting time against vaccine efficacy, side effects, origin, and cash incentives.

**Key Findings:** Longer waiting times significantly reduced stated vaccine choice (β = -0.30, SE = 0.028, p < 0.001). Moving from 0 to 6 months reduced predicted vaccination probability by 17.3 percentage points (from 71.3% to 54.0%). Substantial heterogeneity in waiting-time sensitivity was observed (random-coefficient SD = 1.27), with exploratory moderation by institutional trust.

**Significance for Behavioral Medicine:** This study contributes to behavioral medicine by:
1. Quantifying sensitivity to waiting time as a modifiable service-delivery feature of preventive health choice
2. Characterizing heterogeneity in delay sensitivity through random-coefficient mixed logit and institutional trust subgroups
3. Placing waiting time on the same preference scale as clinically relevant vaccine attributes

**Fit with JBM:** The study aligns with JBM's focus on behavioral determinants of health by treating waiting time not merely as an administrative friction but as a behavioral factor that shapes preventive decision-making under uncertainty. The findings have implications for designing timely and equitable vaccination strategies.

Dear Editor,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, "Delay Sensitivity in Stated Vaccine Choice: Evidence from a Discrete Choice Experiment in Wuhan, China," for consideration in the Journal of Behavioral Medicine.

This study examines whether waiting time before vaccination—a potentially modifiable feature of health-service delivery—shapes preventive health decision-making. Using a discrete choice experiment administered to 1,027 adults in Wuhan, China, we find that longer waiting times significantly reduce stated vaccine choice. In the primary model, increasing waiting from 0 to 6 months reduced predicted vaccination probability from 71.3% to 54.0%, a 17.3-percentage-point reduction. We also find substantial heterogeneity in waiting-time sensitivity, while moderation by institutional trust is mixed and treated as exploratory.

We believe the manuscript is well suited to the Journal of Behavioral Medicine because it focuses on how a concrete feature of health-service delivery influences preventive decision-making. Rather than treating waiting time solely as an administrative constraint, the study shows that service timing remains behaviorally consequential after accounting for major vaccine attributes. The findings provide a rationale for prospectively testing reduced-delay access and other service-design interventions against actual vaccination uptake, while the manuscript carefully distinguishes stated-choice effects from real-world uptake and frames the underlying psychological mechanisms as questions for future testing.

All authors have approved the manuscript and declare no conflicts of interest. The study received appropriate institutional ethical review, and all participants provided informed consent. This manuscript is original, has not been published previously, and is not under consideration elsewhere.

Thank you for considering our manuscript.
